There is a scene in Jacques Tati's film _Playtime_, that I have always loved. In it, Tati's Mr. Hulot has just exited a city bus and runs into an old army buddy who invites him into see his new apartment. Hulot and his friend enter the apartment building which is all concrete and glass, with large windows allowing us to see the entirety of the action within. Over the course of many drawn out minutes, Tati uses a minimum of cuts to create a slow tableau of drama, where we witness uncomfortable interactions, secret vices, and humorous juxtapositions. And because Tati filmed his movie in 70mm, when seen properly on the large screen, the building takes up our entire field of view. We are voyeurs standing outside on the street and peering in. "\[O]ur eyes dart around it to find action in the foreground, middle distance, background and half-offscreen. It is difficult sometimes to even know what the subject of a shot is." (Ebert) Through this visual scanning we notice little gags, insights, and peculiarities from the characters and the scenery itself. And as such:

_"Thus although _Playtime_ is stuffed full of gags, most are disperesed quietly throughout the film, and often only on acute observation from the audience [...] Ever present, Tati's humerous moments suggest that all architectural scenes, whether in Tativille or the street outside, have such comic potential, if onlfy we should pay attention to realise this much."_ (Borden 2002, 220)

Much has been written about Tati's use of architecture to comment on the modern world, but I think his use of time is notable here. Is is intensely purposeful. He lingers on a shot for a long time, what almost feels too long to us, and lets the action play out before us. He has characters repeat certain behaviors of movements beyond what seems reasonable. His lingering asks for patience from the audience, but that patience is rewarded with an insightful humor. It is a humor of recognition. A humor that says "ah yes, I see similar things in the world around me." 

As designers we are too often afraid of wasting the time of our users. Cinema has become a medium of fast cuts, quick action, and non-stop movement. As film-times have lengthened the average amount of time between cuts has shortened (cite?). Video game designers have optimized their code in such a way so that there no longer needs to be a loading screen between areas. The infamous door animations of "Resident Evil" replaced by Link barging into every new space in "Breath of the Wild," hardly pausing to adjust his tunic. (Examples from other media necessary? It is an attitude of attentional lingering.

Tati himself bemoaned the attention-span of much of his audience. "But if you have to get rid of all of the repetitions, and if you have to make it so that everyone can hear absolutely everything, then it would no longer be Play Time. I should have just made a short film, and then everyone
would have been happy." (Tati 2017, 124) We are asking a lot of our users, viewers, and players when we ask of their time. We are asking them to trust us with a valuable resource. They have a million things they could be doing (or may be doing simultaneously) and to ask for an attitude of directed attention can be bold. But maybe in asking for this time we can shift how time is even considered.

## Examples

We can see this happening in other creative media

- Recorded Music - Eno's "Music for Airports" (and ambient music in general) seeks to bring us in and out of kairos by creating music that can shift from foreground to background perception.
- Live Music - Cage's "As Slow as Possible" and "4:33" do two opposite things. "As slow as possible" forces us to engage with the breadth of time, while "4:33" focuses us on the immediate sounds around us.
- Theatre - Einstein on the Beach uses repetitive motifs and actions
- Film - Tati, but also films like Warhol's "Empire", Marclay's "Clock," and the slow cinema movement exemplified by Andrei Tarkovsky

It also has been experimented with in some games

- "The Longing" crystalizes the human-scale of time in a game mechanic. 
- "Vesper5" makes a game of a small (hopefully daily) intentional action.
- Ritual of the Moon does... something
